Suzhou numerals (蘇州碼子, also called 花碼) are a merchants' shorthand that grew out of [counting rods](https://karlnorling.github.io/counting-rods/). They were written on price tags, invoices and pharmacy labels, and they still turn up in Hong Kong markets.

## The digits

Zero is a circle, one to three are bars, and four is a cross. Five to nine take zero to four and put an upright bar on top: five is a bar over a circle, six to eight a bar over one to three flat strokes, and nine a bar over the cross.

## Neighbouring 1s, 2s and 3s

Three upright bars could be 3, or 2 next to 1, or 1, 1, 1. So in a run of 1s, 2s and 3s, writers turn every second one flat, borrowing the ordinary characters 一, 二 and 三. 21 is written 〢一. `alternate: false` turns the rule off.

## Decimals and negatives

On a price tag, a second line names the magnitude and unit of the first digit, such as 十元 for tens of dollars, and that fixes where the decimal point goes. This library puts a dot on the baseline instead. Negative numbers get a diagonal stroke across the last digit, the sign used on counting rods.

<Aside>
  Unicode has the Suzhou digits as 〡 to 〩 (U+3021–3029), plus 〸, 〹 and 〺 for 10, 20 and 30.
  This library draws its own strokes, so the output doesn't depend on fonts.
